Discovery Call Playbook

How to approach a merchant, what to ask first, and the mini-scripts to get your foot in the door — for any product. The big idea: discover before you pitch, keep it simple, and get in front of them.

The mindset — get your foot in the door

"Get your foot in the door. Don't oversell. Don't sell him a full POS if he didn't tell you that's what he's looking for. Get the account and the processing, and upsell later — if he even needs it."

Most deals are lost by giving too much, too soon. When you dump every feature on a merchant, they think "man, I'd have to change everything" — and they either stay where they are or just switch to another processor. Your job on the first interaction isn't to pitch the whole catalog; it's to learn enough to point them in one direction and earn the next conversation.

1Lead with their reason, not your products

Think about why they entertained you in the first place. There's a pain or a trigger — anchor everything to that, not to your feature list.

2Discover before you pitch

If your email or pitch is getting long, it's a sign you don't know enough yet. Get the details first; the right solution becomes obvious once you do.

3Get in front of them

Discovery questions land far better on a call or in person than in a long email. "Send me an email" often means a slow ghost — push for 10 minutes live.

4Keep it simple

Especially with old-school owners. Don't open complex cans of worms (inventory, integrations) unless it's a real pain point they asked you to solve.

Foot-in-the-door ladder: get the account & processing first → prove you're easy to work with → upsell hardware/POS and add-ons later, only if and when they need them. A standalone-terminal merchant doesn't need a full POS pitch on day one.

Are you actually in a sales process?

"Hounding people to close, offering discounts, and calling them every day isn't a sales process — it's activity padding for reps who don't know whether they're in a real process or not."

Before you start chasing a deal, get honest about where you really are. There's a big difference between still getting to know each other and having mutually agreed to move forward. A real sales process is mutual — both sides know the next step and have agreed to it. If only you're pushing, you don't have a process; you have a hope.

The question that tells you where you stand

"Are we just getting to know each other still, or have we mutually entered a sales process together? If we have — here's what we usually see happen next on your side. How does it actually work from your perspective?" Ask it out loud. Their answer tells you whether there's a real opportunity, who else is involved, and what the next step truly is — or that you're still earning the right to one.

Real process

Both sides agree there's a fit worth exploring, know the next step, and have a rough timeline and decision process. You're building a mutual plan — not chasing.

Activity padding (not a process)

Daily "just checking in" calls, surprise discounts, pushing to close on your timeline. It feels busy, but it's a sign you never confirmed they're actually in it with you.

What to do instead of chasing: confirm mutual commitment, then map the steps together — "What would you need to see? Who else weighs in? What's a realistic timeline?" If they won't co-build that with you, you're not in a process yet — keep it light and keep qualifying.

What to ask first — the short list

You only need a handful of answers to know where to take it. Get these (ideally live), and save the deep stuff for later.

What are you using now?

Standalone card terminal, or a full POS system? Who's your current processor?

Why: tells you if this is a simple processing switch or a system sale. Old-school + a big bank usually = just a terminal.

What made you open to a conversation?

What's the pain or trigger — fees, fraud, a clunky system, bad support?

Why: that pain is your whole pitch. Solve it; don't bury it under features.

Terminal or full system?

Are you just looking to take payments, or do you want a full POS?

Why: if they only want a terminal, all the feature/integration questions don't matter yet — don't ask them.

Do you want to eliminate your processing fees?

Would you rather keep paying fees, or pass them to the customer (dual pricing / cash discount) and get to 0%?

Why: this single question shapes the whole proposal and is a strong reason to switch.

Can I see a recent statement?

To match or beat your current rate, I'll take a quick look at a statement.

Why: gets you the real numbers and a clear, no-risk reason for the next touch.

Only after they say they want a full system do you go deeper: which features matter most (reporting, inventory, scheduling, loyalty, online ordering, integrations), buy vs. $0-down placement, etc. Don't lead with these — they overwhelm.

Mini-scripts

Teal blocks are what you say; italics are the coaching note. Swap in the merchant's name and their actual pain.

Opener (in person / phone)

"Quick question — how are you taking cards right now? … Got it. What made you open to looking at something different?" Curiosity first. Let them name the pain. You're qualifying, not pitching.

When they say "just email me"

"Happy to. Honestly, to point you the right way instead of sending a wall of options, the fastest thing is 10 minutes on the phone — are you around [day/time]? I'll come with one or two ideas that actually fit." "Send me an email" is often a soft ghost. Always try to convert it to a live conversation. If they still want email, send the lean one below.

Anchor to their pain (not your catalog)

"You mentioned [their pain — e.g., fees creeping up, the system going down, slow support]. The right setup fixes exactly that, and ideally we get you a better rate than you've got now. Let me ask a couple quick things so I don't oversell you." One sentence of value tied to their words. Then back to discovery.

Keep it simple / don't overwhelm

"If what you've got works, we don't need to reinvent it — let's just fix the part that's costing you and make sure you're not overpaying. We can always add more later." Reassure the change-averse owner. Low-commitment beats a big rip-and-replace pitch.

Lock the next step

"Tell you what — send me a recent statement and let's grab 10 minutes [day/time]. I'll show you exactly where I can help and what it'd look like. No pressure either way." Always leave with a concrete next action: a call booked and/or a statement coming.

The email: lean vs. too much

If they insist on email, keep it short, anchor to their pain, ask two questions max, and push for a call. The long version below feels thorough — but it makes the merchant feel like they have to change everything.

⚠ Too much — overwhelms
Hi Peter, A few systems come to mind that could be a fit… but then a long list: full POS vs terminal? customer-facing display, handheld, cash drawer, printer, barcode scanner? buy or $0-down placement? reporting, invoicing, inventory, label printing, scheduling/payroll, website, advertising, loyalty, gift cards? any third-party integrations? cash discount or surcharge? … Result: he doesn't know enough to answer all that, feels like a big project, and goes quiet.
✓ Lean — gets you the next step
Subject: Great meeting you, Peter Hi Peter — great meeting you. You mentioned your processing costs have crept up and the current setup's been a bit of a headache; I think we can fix both and, ideally, beat what you're paying now. Two quick things so I point you the right way: 1) Are you running a standalone terminal, or a full POS today? 2) Want to keep paying card fees, or look at eliminating them (customer covers the fee)? Fastest path is 10 minutes by phone — are you around [day/time]? I'll keep it simple and bring one or two options that fit. Best, [Name]

Proven openers — Sandler & Challenger

Steal these and swap the [brackets]. Sandler = low-pressure and disarming — take the pressure off so they lower their guard. Challenger = lead with an insight that reframes how they think about their cost, then earn the meeting.

Disarming "probably not a fit" email Sandler

Subject: Probably not a fit — but worth a 9-minute look? Hi [Name], I'll be straight with you — I don't know yet whether I can beat what you've got, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But most [business type] owners are surprised by two things on their statement: the non-qualified/junk fees, and their real effective rate. Send me a recent statement and I'll tell you honestly whether there's room to help or not. No pitch either way. Worth a quick look? [Your name] Why it works: removing the pressure ("maybe we're not a fit") disarms them — you're the rare rep who isn't pushing, so they engage.

Upfront-contract voicemail / call Sandler

"Hi [Name], it's [Your name] with NextPay. I'm not calling to pitch you — I'm calling to find out if a 10-minute conversation even makes sense. If it does, great; if not, we both move on, no hard feelings. Fair enough? Reach me at [number]." Why it works: the upfront contract sets expectations and gives them an easy "no," which paradoxically makes "yes" easier.

The "effective rate" reframe email Challenger

Subject: The number most owners read wrong on their statement Hi [Name], quick insight: most owners judge a processor by the rate on the homepage — but the number that actually decides what you pay is your effective rate (total fees ÷ total volume). It's usually 0.5–1% higher than people expect, and that gap is real money. Takes me 5 minutes to read your statement and show you exactly where it's going. Want me to run yours? [Your name] Why it works: you teach them something new about their own bill (Challenger) instead of pitching — the insight is the hook.

Industry-insight email Challenger

Subject: A pattern I keep seeing with [industry] owners Hi [Name], something I run into a lot with [tile shops / restaurants / salons]: [specific pain — e.g., paying the highest fee tier on most transactions, or a system that slows down at the counter]. Most owners don't realize how much it's quietly costing them. There's a cleaner way to handle that — usually simpler and cheaper. Worth 10 minutes to show you what it'd look like for [business]? [Your name] Why it works: a tailored insight tied to their world (Challenger "teach + tailor") earns attention faster than features.

Re-engage a quiet lead (negative reverse) Sandler

"Hi [Name] — haven't heard back, which usually means one of two things: the timing's off, or it's just not a priority right now. Honestly, either is fine. Should I close this out, or is it worth a quick check-in in [month]? Whatever you tell me helps." Why it works: giving them permission to say "no" (negative reverse) often unsticks a ghost and gets a real answer.
Pick your lane by the merchant: price-shopper or skeptical → Sandler (disarm, no pressure). Busy/confident owner who thinks they've got it handled → Challenger (lead with an insight that reframes their cost). Either way: short, one idea, push for a live conversation.

Match the product to the merchant

Read the room, then point — don't present everything. A few signals:

Old-school / wants simple

Lead with an easy, familiar system and a clean signup. Simpler is better — fewer decisions, less to learn.

Specific pain (fees, downtime, support)

Solve that one thing first (e.g., dual pricing to kill the fees, a more reliable setup, real local support). Everything else is secondary.

Just needs to take payments

A terminal + processing is the foot in the door. Don't sell a full POS unless they ask. Upsell later.

Genuinely wants a full system

Now go feature-deep — and use the tools: Build a config or run the quiz to match them.

Prep tip: create your own merchant application and run through it once so the signup is second nature when you're live with a prospect. Then link the rest of what we offer from the website rather than listing it in the room.

Do / Don't

Do

  • Ask why they're open to looking — anchor to that pain
  • Get them on a call or in person
  • Keep it simple; offer the smallest thing that solves the problem
  • Get the account & processing first, then upsell
  • Ask if they want to eliminate processing fees (dual pricing)
  • Always leave with a next step (call booked / statement coming)

Don't

  • Dump the whole feature list in a long email
  • Sell a full POS to someone who only wanted a terminal
  • Open complex cans of worms (inventory, integrations) too early
  • Lead with rates if they care more about safety/ease
  • Rely on "I'll email you" — that's where deals go to ghost
  • Make a change-averse owner feel they must replace everything
